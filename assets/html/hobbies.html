Quantum Computing

This area of nascent technology appears unapproachable for those unfamiliar with the technology. The easiest way to picture it, at least for me, is a rotation. Imagine spinning a circle, a sphere, or a hypersphere (a ball in four or more dimensions) so that its north pole ends up pointing at the correct answer. Each qubit's output is tied to the rotation of this sphere along one dimension and connects that rotation to the final result. The YouTube channel 3Blue1Brown has an excellent brief explainer below that includes wonderful visuals.

While the list of widely available algorithms for quantum computers is somewhat limited, I was able to teach myself to a reasonably fine-grain level with Quantum Computing: An Applied Approach by Jack D. Hidary and Programming Quantum Computers: Essential Algorithms and Code Samples by Eric R. Johnston, Nic Harrigan, and Mercedes Gimeno-Segovia.

For an engineering-focused perspective on where this technology is likely to go next, see my opinion on the future of quantum computing.

Dungeons and Dragons

I started playing the 5th edition of this tabletop game in 2023 to experience a classic game and join the nerd culture in Austin, TX, and haven't looked back! Not only is it a near-infinite source of potential entertainment, but it has also led to new friendships.

For those looking for new resources to get started in the Austin area, I would recommend Tribe Comics and Games. With regular game nights for both D&D and Warhammer, it is a great entry point for both hobbies in the Austin area. Regardless of whether you have ten minutes or ten years of experience, it is a welcoming environment to enjoy a strong communal hobby.


History

Why

I have two main reasons that drew me into history: academic interest and personal curiosity.

"History doesn’t repeat itself, but it does rhyme." — Mark Twain .

Academically, it is interesting to know how historical events decades or centuries ago still have ripples impacting the world today. While history isn't a perfect representation of current events, it does allow us to figure out trends we should expect to see. Many would think that concerns around automation negatively impacting the labor market is a uniquely 21st century problem, but the Luddites had similar concerns around automated looms. The Luddite movement actually was the origin of the word sabotage.

My curiosity was then intrigued by people and events that tend to buck the historical trends that we would expect to see. While understanding trends is good to understand what to expect going forward, it is also healthy to be curious about when those trends do not hold. These unusual events could have a number of potential components making them more complicated, like:

  • Ties to the supernatural, like the Casket girls, immigrant girls who could be the first "vampires" in the United States. While most serious historians don't genuinely believe that New Orleans imported vampires from Europe, it is interesting to see how the supernatural collides with verifiable history.
  • Comical, like when trading onion futures became illegal due to the "Onion King".
  • Ironic, like the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission accidentally making an extremely dangerous lapel pin in 1974. Apparently, in addition to being sharp, it also contained lead paint and was a choking hazard for children. It would appear that one dangerous design decision was not ironic enough.
  • Seemingly-anachronistic, like how suitcases got wheels AFTER humanity landed on the moon.
